Hooke's law and the spring constant

Aim
  1. To verify Hooke's law for a simple spring.
  2. To measure the spring constant of a spring.

Why it matters in medicine

Muscles, tendons and ligaments

Soft tissues stretch under load and recover, much like a spring. Their stiffness shows how much force a joint can take before it is injured.

Prosthetics and orthopaedic supports

Artificial limbs, braces and splints use elastic parts chosen for the right stiffness, so they give support without being too rigid.

Mechanical properties of bone

Under small loads bone deforms elastically. Beyond its limit it deforms permanently or fractures, the same idea as a spring's elastic limit.

Theory

A load hung on a spring pulls it longer. Hooke's law states that, within the elastic limit, the force on a spring is directly proportional to its extension.

F = k ΔL

F is the stretching force, ΔL the extension (the increase in length) and k the spring constant: the force needed for each unit of extension. A stiffer spring has a larger k.

Load m

The mass hung on the spring. Its weight is the stretching force, F = mg.

Extension ΔL

Pointer reading with the load minus the reading with no load.

Spring constant k

k = F / ΔL: in N/m (SI) or dyne/cm (CGS).

Elastic limit

The largest load after which the spring still returns to its original length.

From the law to a straight-line graph

  1. The stretching force is the weight of the load:F = m g
  2. Hooke's law givesm g = k ΔL
  3. Rearranged with ΔL on its own:ΔL = (g / k) · m
ΔL = (g / k) · m
y = S · x

Plot ΔL on the y-axis against m on the x-axis. A straight line through the origin verifies Hooke's law. Its slope is S = g / k, so

k = g · 1/S

Units

SI
F in N, ΔL in m: k in N/m
CGS (used in this lab)
F in dyne, ΔL in cm: k in dyne/cm

1 dyne = 1 g·cm/s². With m in grams and g = 980 cm/s², mg comes out in dynes. For comparison only: 1 N/m = 1000 dyne/cm.

Method

Apparatus

Steps

Good practice
  • Read at eye level. Look straight across at the pointer so the reading has no parallax error.
  • Wait for it to settle. A new load sets the spring bouncing; read only when the pointer is still.
  • Add masses gently. Lower each slotted mass onto the hanger rather than dropping it.
  • Stay within the elastic limit. Too large a load stretches the spring permanently and the readings no longer follow Hooke's law.
  • Check on the way down. Removing the masses one by one should bring the pointer back to the same readings.

Calculation

1
Extension from the zero reading ΔL = reading − initial, e.g. 9.6 − 4.0 = 5.6 cm
2
Slope from two points on the line, with y = ΔL and x = m S = (y₂ − y₁) / (x₂ − x₁) = (5.6 − 1.4) / (80 − 20) = 4.2 / 60 = 0.07 cm/g
3
Convert g to cm/s² to match centimetres and grams g = 9.8 m/s² × 100 = 980 cm/s²
4
Substitute K = g · 1/S = 980 × 1/0.07 = 980 / 0.07
✓
Result Spring constant K = 14 000 dyne/cm For comparison: 1 N/m = 1000 dyne/cm, so this is 14 N/m.

Why dyne/cm?

g in cm/s², 1/S in g/cm
g · 1/S → (g·cm/s²) / cm
=
1 dyne = 1 g·cm/s²
K in dyne/cm

Real readings scatter a little: a pointer read to the nearest millimetre can be 0.05 cm out either way. Taking the slope from two points far apart on the best line keeps that error small.

Common slips

Using g = 9.8

With ΔL in cm and m in g, g must be 980 cm/s². Using 9.8 gives K = 140, which is 100 times too small.

Slope upside down

ΔL is on the y-axis, so S = ΔL / Δm. Taking Δm / ΔL = 60 / 4.2 = 14.3 would give K = 68.6, far off.

Forgetting the zero reading

The pointer reading is not the extension. Using 9.6 cm at 80 g instead of 5.6 cm gives K ≈ 8170 instead of 14 000.

Passing the elastic limit

Overloading stretches the spring permanently. The points then curve away from the line and the zero reading shifts.
